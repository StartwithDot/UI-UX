# the start of the week

## LEARN — 60 minutes

Read on application strategy: what a referral is worth relative to a cold application, what a hiring manager sees in an applicant tracking system, and why applying to 200 roles produces fewer interviews than applying to 20 properly.

→ **Commit** `students/UX{n}/week24/session1-learning.md`

## DO — 3 hours

### B4.1 — Target list

**Method**
1. Twenty companies. Real ones, with real openings or real reasons to speculatively approach.
2. For each: why you, why them, and who the actual person is who would read your application.
3. **Rank by fit, not by prestige.** A team where the users have no choice about using the product is a better fit for your five projects than a consumer app company, and your portfolio will read as strong there and generic elsewhere.
4. For each, note one specific thing about their product or their team that you could mention credibly.

**Done when**
- [ ] Twenty companies with real openings or real reasons
- [ ] A named person or role identified per company where possible
- [ ] Ranked by fit with a stated reason
- [ ] One specific, credible detail noted per company

→ `students/UX{n}/week24/B4-1-targets.md`

### B4.2 — Materials

**Method**
1. A cover note template that is four sentences: what you do, why them specifically, the one project most relevant to them, and a link.
2. **Four sentences.** Nobody reads a page.
3. Customise the second sentence per application. Genuinely — a generic "I admire your commitment to innovation" is worse than nothing.
4. Have the portfolio link, résumé PDF, and LinkedIn all consistent. A mismatch between them is the first thing that gets noticed.

**Worked example**

> "I'm a product designer working on services people have no choice about using — public health, benefits, and gig work.
>
> I'm writing to you because your driver-earnings screen is the clearest breakdown of deductions I have seen in this market, and it is the exact problem I spent four weeks on in my capstone.
>
> That project is the second one on my site: contextual research with five riders, a dispute flow, and testing that took task completion from 2/5 to 5/5.
>
> Portfolio: priyamenon.design — happy to walk through any of it."

**Done when**
- [ ] A four-sentence template
- [ ] The second sentence genuinely customisable per company
- [ ] Portfolio, résumé, and LinkedIn all consistent
- [ ] No generic praise anywhere

→ `students/UX{n}/week24/B4-2-materials.md`

### B4.4 — Outreach

**Method**
1. Five messages to designers working where you want to work. Not asking for a job — asking one specific question about their work.
2. Two contributions to a public design conversation. A comment with substance, a written response to an article, a critique posted publicly. Visible reasoning is a hiring signal.
3. Join one community and be useful in it once.

**Done when**

# late in the week

## Mock interview loop — 3 hours

Run as a real loop, by someone who has not been your reviewer, ideally an external designer.

| Stage | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Recruiter screen | 20 min | Motivation, background, salary expectation |
| Portfolio review | 45 min | Two projects, with questions throughout |
| Whiteboard | 45 min | A prompt you have not seen |
| Craft interview | 30 min | Given a design, critique it and specify a fix |
| Behavioural | 30 min | Conflict, failure, and how you work |
| Debrief | 10 min | Scored feedback, per stage |

**You get a written score per stage and the feedback is unsoftened.** Better here than in a real loop where you do not find out why you were rejected.

→ `students/UX{n}/week24/B4-mock-loop-feedback.md`

# the end of the week

## Final defense — 90 minutes

Twenty-four weeks in scope, run as an interview rather than as a review.

| Part | Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Portfolio walk | 20 min | Two case studies, with interruptions |
| Whiteboard | 25 min | A fresh prompt, out loud |
| Craft interrogation | 15 min | Numbers. Spacing, contrast, type, motion, tokens. |
| Accessibility | 10 min | Any criterion, any project, and how you verified it |
| Systems | 10 min | Tokens, components, governance |
| Ethics | 5 min | Your position, and the last time you tested it |
| Reflection | 5 min | What you are still bad at |

**The pass condition is not perfection.** It is that you can be given a problem, ask the right questions, reason out loud, defend a decision with evidence, change your mind when shown better evidence, and say honestly where your knowledge ends.

**The two failure modes:** bluffing an answer you do not have, and collapsing when challenged on something you do. Both are graded down. "I don't know, and here is how I would find out" is a passing answer. Inventing a number is not.

# the cohort review

## B4.5 — 90-day plan

**Method**
1. What you do in the next 90 days, week by week, whether or not you have a job.
2. **The skill gaps you know about**, from your six reflections, with what you will do about each and by when.
3. Your continued practice: the drill log continues, teardowns continue, one case study updated per quarter.
4. What you will not do: the shiny distractions. Learning a new tool instead of getting better at the thing you are weak at is the standard trap.
5. A review date, in your own calendar, with the specific question you will ask yourself on it.

**Done when**
- [ ] Week by week for 90 days
- [ ] Every known gap has an action and a date
- [ ] A continued practice commitment with a frequency
- [ ] An explicit list of what you are avoiding
- [ ] A review date in a real calendar

→ `students/UX{n}/week24/B4-5-90-day-plan.md`
