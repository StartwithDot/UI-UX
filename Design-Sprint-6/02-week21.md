# the start of the week

## LEARN — 90 minutes

| Source | What exactly | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **Real portfolios** | Five case studies by designers currently employed at the level you want | 50 min |
| **`06-case-study-structure.md`** | The full structure and the reasoning behind each part | 25 min |
| **Your own five projects** | Ranked honestly by evidence strength | 15 min |

**Read the five critically.** Most portfolio case studies are process theatre — a double diamond diagram, some sticky notes, a persona nobody used, and then finished screens with no explanation of how one became the other. Note which of the five you actually believed, and why.

→ **Commit** `students/UX{n}/week21/session1-learning.md`

## DO — 3 hours

### B1.1 — Choose three

**Method**
1. Rank all five projects on: strength of evidence · complexity of the problem · how much of the reasoning is documented · whether you can defend every decision · whether it shows something the others do not.
2. Choose three. The capstone is almost certainly one.
3. **Write down why you cut the other two.** Honestly. Weak research, derivative design, or a problem too simple to be interesting.
4. Check your three for range: they should not all be the same type of problem. A booking flow, a booking flow, and a booking flow shows one skill three times.

**Done when**
- [ ] All five ranked on all five criteria
- [ ] Three chosen, with the reason
- [ ] The two cuts explained honestly
- [ ] The three demonstrate genuinely different skills

→ `students/UX{n}/week21/B1-1-selection.md`

### B1.2 — Structure each one

**Method**
Plan all three before writing any. Per case study:
- **The hook** — one sentence on what the problem was and what changed. This is the only thing most readers will read.
- **Context** — 100 words maximum. What, who for, your role, when, how long.
- **The problem** — with evidence. Numbers if you have them.
- **What you found** — the finding that changed your direction. Not all your findings; the one that mattered.
- **The design** — decisions, not a tour. Every screenshot has a reason for being there.
- **A specific tradeoff** — what you chose against, and what it cost.
- **What testing changed** — one before and after.
- **The outcome** — measured if possible, honestly estimated if not.
- **What you would do differently** — a real answer.

**The hook does the work.** If someone reads only the hook and looks at one image, they should still understand what you did.

**Worked example** — a hook:

> ❌ "A UX case study exploring the redesign of a hospital appointment booking experience."
>
> ✅ "Patients at a district hospital were arriving on the wrong day because the SMS confirmation gave the date in a format most of them could not read. I redesigned the booking flow and the message. In testing, wrong-day arrivals went from 4 of 5 participants to 0 of 5."
>
> The second one names a specific failure, a specific cause, and a specific result. It also tells you the designer went and found out why, rather than assuming the interface was the problem.

**Done when**
- [ ] All three planned before any writing
- [ ] Each hook is one or two sentences and contains a specific result
- [ ] Every section has its content decided
- [ ] Every planned screenshot has a stated reason for inclusion

→ `students/UX{n}/week21/B1-2-structure.md`

## DO — 4 hours

### B1.3 — Case study one

Write it fully. Sections in order.

**Rules:**
- 800 to 1,200 words. Longer does not get read.
- Every claim about users has evidence attached.
- Every screenshot has a caption that says what to notice. An uncaptioned screenshot is decoration.
- Show one thing that did not work. **This is not a weakness** — it is the strongest signal available that the work was real.
- No process diagrams unless the process itself was the innovation.
- Say what you did versus what a team did. Overclaiming is the fastest way to fail a portfolio review.

### the week's critique — 90 minutes

**The ninety-second test.** A peer reads your case study for ninety seconds, then closes it and tells you: what the problem was, what you did, and what happened. Whatever they cannot answer is a structural failure in your writing, not a failure of their attention.

# the end of the week

## LEARN — 45 minutes

Read anything on editing — the standard advice is to cut adverbs, cut hedges, and cut the first paragraph. Apply it to your own three.

→ **Commit** `session5-learning.md`

## DO — 3 hours

### B1.6 — The cut pass

**Method**
1. Cut each case study by 30%. Not 10%. Thirty.
2. What goes first: process description, hedging language ("I felt that perhaps", "we tried to"), anything explaining what a heuristic evaluation is, and every sentence that says what you were about to do rather than what you did.
3. Read each one aloud. Every sentence you stumble over gets rewritten.
4. **Check every claim.** Anything you cannot support with something in your repository gets removed or softened to what you can support. An interviewer who catches one unsupported claim discounts all of them.

**Done when**
- [ ] All three cut by 30%, word counts recorded before and after
- [ ] Zero hedging language
- [ ] Zero explanations of standard methods
- [ ] Read aloud, stumbles fixed
- [ ] Every claim traceable to something in the repo

→ `students/UX{n}/week21/B1-6-edited/`
