## DO — 3 hours

### B2.1 — Structure

**Method**
1. Decide the pages. Home, three case studies, about, and a contact route. That is usually all.
2. Decide what the home page must do in ten seconds: who you are, what kind of work you do, and one reason to keep reading.
3. Decide the navigation. Someone landing on a case study from a link must be able to get to the rest.
4. **Decide what you are not including.** No dribbble shots, no unfinished concepts, no "passion projects" with no problem behind them. Every extra weak item lowers the average, and the average is what gets judged.

**Done when**
- [ ] Page list decided and minimal
- [ ] The ten-second job of the home page stated
- [ ] Navigation works from any entry point
- [ ] An explicit list of what you are leaving out

→ `students/UX{n}/week22/B2-1-structure.md`

### B2.2 — Home page

**Method**
1. Above the fold: your name, what you do, and the three projects. Nothing else.
2. **No "welcome to my portfolio".** No slow reveal animation. No scroll-jacking. No cursor effects. Every one of those costs you a reader.
3. Each project gets a card with the hook — the sentence from B1.2 — not a title like "Project 1".
4. One sentence on what kind of problem you want to work on. This is the line that makes a hiring manager think of a specific team.

**Worked example**

> ❌ "Hi, I'm Priya! 👋 I'm a passionate UX designer who loves creating delightful user experiences."
>
> ✅ "Priya Menon — product designer, Bangalore. I work on services where the users have no choice about using them: public health, benefits, and gig work. Three case studies below, each with the research behind it."
>
> The second one is specific, it is not about how much she loves design, and it tells a hiring manager in the public-sector space that she is the candidate for them.

**Done when**
- [ ] Name, discipline, and three projects above the fold
- [ ] No welcome message, no animation on entry
- [ ] Each project shows its hook
- [ ] One specific line about the work you want
- [ ] Nothing on the page that does not help someone decide to keep reading

→ `students/UX{n}/week22/B2-2-home.md`

# Tuesday

## DO — 5 hours

### B2.3 — Build it

**Method**
1. Build and deploy. Live URL by end of day.
2. **Use your own design system.** You built one for four sprints, and a portfolio that ignores it is a portfolio that says the system was an academic exercise.
3. Test on a real phone, not a simulator.
4. Test on a slow connection. Throttle it to 3G in devtools and see what happens.

**Done when**
- [ ] Live URL, deployed
- [ ] Your own tokens and components used
- [ ] Tested on a real phone
- [ ] Tested on a throttled connection, with a note on load time
- [ ] All three case studies readable end to end

# Wednesday

## LEARN — 45 minutes

Read the conventions for a one-page design résumé and for a LinkedIn profile that is not written in the third person. Look at three of each from people at the level you want.

→ **Commit** `day3-learning.md`

## DO — 3 hours

### B2.4 — Résumé

**Method**
1. One page. One.
2. What you did, with what result. Not what you were responsible for.
3. Every bullet: action, method, outcome. If there is no outcome, there is no bullet.
4. The program goes on it as what it is — twenty-four weeks of project work with defended deliverables. Do not dress it up as employment, and do not undersell it either.
5. Plain, machine-readable. Many applications go through parsers that cannot read a two-column layout with icons.

**Worked example**

> ❌ "Responsible for redesigning the appointment booking user experience."
>
> ✅ "Redesigned a hospital appointment booking flow after contextual research with 5 patients and 2 receptionists; usability testing showed wrong-day arrivals fall from 4/5 to 0/5 participants."

**Done when**
- [ ] One page
- [ ] Every bullet has an action, a method, and an outcome
- [ ] No "responsible for"
- [ ] The program described accurately
- [ ] Parses correctly as plain text

→ `students/UX{n}/week22/B2-4-resume.pdf`

# Thursday

## DO — 3 hours (plus external review)

### B2.6 — Accessibility and performance

**Method**
1. Run axe or Lighthouse. Fix everything.
2. Keyboard-navigate the whole site. Visible focus everywhere.
3. Test with a screen reader. Reading order, alt text, heading hierarchy.
4. Every image has real alt text, and decorative images have empty alt.
5. Load time under 3 seconds on a throttled connection.
6. **Write the accessibility statement for your own site**, in the same format you have used for four sprints, including anything that still fails.

**The stakes are specific here.** You have spent six months claiming accessibility matters. An inaccessible portfolio is a portfolio that disproves its own case studies, and any competent interviewer will check.

**Done when**
- [ ] Automated audit clean
- [ ] Full keyboard walkthrough, focus visible throughout
- [ ] Screen reader tested, reading order correct
- [ ] Alt text on every image, empty on decorative ones
- [ ] Under 3 seconds throttled
- [ ] An accessibility statement published, including known issues

→ `students/UX{n}/week22/B2-6-a11y.md`

### External review — Thursday afternoon

A designer or hiring manager who is not part of this program reviews your portfolio. They will spend ninety seconds, then talk.

**Your job is to take notes and not argue.** Every "I couldn't tell what this project was about" is a fact about your portfolio, not an opinion you can correct.

# Friday

## DO — 4 hours

### B2.7 — Fix it

**Method**
1. Every piece of feedback gets a decision: fix, defer, or reject.
2. For rejections, write the reason. Rejecting feedback is legitimate — but only with a reason you would say out loud to the reviewer.
3. Fix everything in the fix list.
4. **Re-test the ninety-second comprehension** with a different person afterwards.

**Done when**
- [ ] Every piece of feedback has a decision
- [ ] Rejections justified in writing
- [ ] Fix list complete
- [ ] Re-tested with someone new, and they can state the problem and outcome of each project

→ `students/UX{n}/week22/B2-7-revisions.md`
