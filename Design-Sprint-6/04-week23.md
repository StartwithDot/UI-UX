# Monday

## LEARN — 75 minutes

Read `07-interview-question-bank.md` in full. 150 questions, each with a note on what the interviewer is actually testing — which is almost never the surface question.

→ **Commit** `students/UX{n}/week23/day1-learning.md`

## DO — 4 hours

### B3.1 — Prepared answers

**Method**
1. Write answers to 40 questions from the bank. Cover: your process, a specific project, conflict, failure, accessibility, systems, ethics, and craft.
2. **Write them, then say them out loud, then cut them in half.** A written answer is always too long as a spoken one.
3. For every project question, use a specific example with a specific number. "We improved the flow" is not an answer; "task completion went from 2 of 5 to 5 of 5 in the second round" is.
4. For the failure and conflict questions, use real ones from the last twenty-three weeks. You have five failure injections, four post mortems, and a documented principle conflict. Use them.
5. **Prepare the three questions you are worst at**, specifically. You know which they are. For most people it is "why did you leave your last field", "what is your weakness", and "what would you have done if the business overruled you".

**Worked example** — a weakness answer that works:

> ❌ "I'm a perfectionist and I care too much about details."
>
> ✅ "I under-communicate while I'm still working something out. In my capstone I sat on a research finding for four days that invalidated part of my own design, because I wanted to have a solution before I raised it. By the time I did, I had lost three days I could have used. What I do now is raise the problem the day I see it, even without the answer — I write the finding, state that I do not yet know what to do about it, and give a date for when I will. It works, and I still have to make myself do it."

That answer names a real fault, gives a specific instance with a cost, describes a concrete correction, and admits the correction is effortful. All four parts are necessary.

**Done when**
- [ ] 40 answers written
- [ ] Every one said aloud and cut
- [ ] Every project answer has a specific number
- [ ] Failure and conflict answers use real events from the program
- [ ] The three you are worst at are drilled specifically

# Tuesday

## LEARN — 45 minutes

Read how a design whiteboard or app-critique exercise is actually assessed. The assessment is almost never the solution — it is whether you asked about constraints, whether you named your assumptions, and whether you could be redirected without falling apart.

→ **Commit** `day2-learning.md`

## DO — 4 hours

### B3.2 — Whiteboard exercise one

**Method — the sequence that works, 45 minutes:**
1. **Clarify, 5 min.** Who is the user, what is the business context, what platform, what is out of scope. **Ask before you draw.** Candidates who start sketching in the first minute fail this section regardless of the sketch.
2. **Frame, 5 min.** State the problem in one sentence and get agreement. If they disagree, that is useful information and you have found it in minute ten rather than minute forty.
3. **Diverge, 10 min.** Three approaches, sketched roughly, each described in one sentence.
4. **Choose, 5 min.** Pick one and say why, against the criteria you clarified in step one.
5. **Detail, 15 min.** Sketch the main flow. Say what you are choosing at each screen.
6. **Critique yourself, 5 min.** Name the weaknesses, what you would test, and what you would do next.

**Talk continuously.** The interviewer is assessing your reasoning, and silence gives them nothing to assess. Narrate even the uncertainty: "I'm not sure whether this should be one screen or two, so I'm going to sketch one and note the split as an option."

**Prompt for exercise one:** design a way for a delivery rider to report that an address does not exist.

**Done when**
- [ ] 45 minutes, timed
- [ ] Clarifying questions asked before any sketching
- [ ] Problem framed and agreement sought
- [ ] Three approaches before committing to one
- [ ] Self-critique at the end
- [ ] Recorded, and watched back

→ `students/UX{n}/week23/B3-2-whiteboard-1.md`

# Wednesday

## DO — 5 hours

### B3.4 — Whiteboard exercises three, four, and five

Three more, 45 minutes each, timed and recorded.

**Prompts:**
3. Design an interface for a nurse to record vital signs during a ward round.
4. Design a way for a person with low literacy to prove their identity remotely.
5. Design a dashboard for a small shop owner to understand which products lose money.

**After the fifth, write what changed between one and five.** Usually: fewer minutes before the first question, better framing, and less panic in the middle.

→ `students/UX{n}/week23/B3-4-whiteboards-3-5.md`

### B3.5 — Critique performance

**Method**
1. You are given a product and asked to critique it live, in ten minutes. This is a standard interview exercise.
2. The structure: what it is trying to do · what works and why · what does not work and why · what you would change first and what evidence you would want · what you would need to know before you were confident.
3. **Be specific and be fair.** "The contrast on the secondary text is about 3:1, which fails AA, and it is the text that carries the price" is a critique. "It looks cluttered" is not.
4. Do not perform contempt. Interviewers are watching how you would talk about their product, and by extension how you would talk about a colleague's work.

**Done when**
- [ ] Ten minutes, recorded
- [ ] Every criticism is specific and evidenced
- [ ] Something genuine identified as working
- [ ] A first change named with the evidence you would want
- [ ] Nothing dismissive in the tone

→ `students/UX{n}/week23/B3-5-critique-performance.md`

# Thursday

## DO — 4 hours (plus critique)

### B3.6 — Portfolio walkthrough

**Method**
1. Record a twenty-minute walkthrough of one case study. Then a five-minute version of the same one.
2. **The five-minute version is harder and is the one you will actually be asked for.** Most portfolio reviews are 30 minutes total for three projects plus questions.
3. Watch both back. Count your filler words. Note every place you explained your process instead of your reasoning.
4. Re-record whichever is worse.

**Done when**
- [ ] Twenty-minute version recorded
- [ ] Five-minute version recorded
- [ ] Both watched back with notes
- [ ] Filler words counted
- [ ] One re-recorded after review

→ `students/UX{n}/week23/B3-6-walkthrough/`

### Thursday critique — 90 minutes
Peers run a portfolio review on you and ask hostile questions. Specifically: "how do you know that?", "what would you have done if the research said the opposite?", and "which part of this are you least confident about?"

# Friday

## DO — 3 hours

### B3.7 — Behavioural and negotiation

**Method**
1. Prepare answers for: a disagreement with an engineer · a time you were wrong · a time you had to ship something you were not happy with · how you handle feedback you disagree with · what you do when you have no research budget.
2. **Use STAR** — situation, task, action, result — but do not announce it. It is a structure, not a script.
3. Prepare the salary conversation. Know the actual range for your level and location. Practise saying a number and then stopping talking, which is the part everyone gets wrong.
4. Prepare your own questions for them. Five, and they should be questions whose answers would actually change whether you accepted. "What is the culture like" is not one of those.

**Worked example** — questions that reveal something:

> - "Who was the last designer to leave this team, and why?"
> - "Walk me through the last design decision that got overruled, and how that was handled."
> - "How much of the last quarter's design work was research-informed versus requested by stakeholders?"
> - "What happens when accessibility conflicts with a deadline here?"
> - "What would I be expected to have shipped by the end of month three?"

**Done when**
- [ ] Five behavioural answers, practised aloud
- [ ] Salary range researched, with a number you can say
- [ ] Practised saying the number and stopping
- [ ] Five questions that would genuinely affect your decision
