# Case Study Structure

The structure of a case study and the reason each part exists. You start using it in week 8 (task JR2) and finish three case studies in week 21.

---

## The principle

A case study is an argument that you can be trusted with a problem. The reader is a stranger with ninety seconds, then perhaps twenty minutes. The hook has to carry the argument alone; the rest is the evidence.

Most case studies fail the same way: a double-diamond diagram, some sticky notes, a persona nobody used, then finished screens, with nothing explaining how one became the other. The reader cannot tell what you decided, only what you made.

---

## The nine parts

| # | Part | Length | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The hook** | 1 to 2 sentences | Names a specific failure, a specific cause, and a specific result. The only thing most readers read. |
| 2 | **Context** | Under 100 words | What it was, who for, your role, when, how long. So the reader can judge scale and what is yours. |
| 3 | **The problem, with evidence** | 100 to 150 words | Proves the problem was real before you touched it. Numbers if you have them. |
| 4 | **What you found** | 100 to 150 words | The one finding that changed your direction. Not all of them. |
| 5 | **The design** | 200 to 300 words | Decisions, not a tour. Every screenshot has a caption saying what to notice. |
| 6 | **A specific tradeoff** | 80 to 120 words | What you chose against, and what it cost. Shows judgement, which is what is being hired. |
| 7 | **What testing changed** | 80 to 120 words | One before and after. Shows you can be wrong and respond. |
| 8 | **The outcome** | 60 to 100 words | Measured if possible. If not, honestly estimated and labelled as an estimate. |
| 9 | **What you got wrong, and what you would do differently** | 80 to 120 words | The strongest signal available that the work was real. Nobody includes it; it is the part that gets designers hired. |

**Target 800 to 1,200 words in total.** Longer does not get read.

---

## Part by part

### 1. The hook

> ❌ "A UX case study exploring the redesign of a hospital appointment booking experience."
>
> ✅ "Patients at a district hospital were arriving on the wrong day because the SMS confirmation gave the date in a format most of them could not read. I redesigned the booking flow and the message. In testing, wrong-day arrivals went from 4 of 5 participants to 0 of 5."

Test: if someone reads only the hook and looks at one image, they should understand what you did.

### 2. Context

Say what you did versus what a team did. Overclaiming is the fastest way to fail a portfolio review. For program work: "A four-week project with four other designers, with a fictional client; I owned the research and the error states."

### 3. The problem, with evidence

State the problem as a user problem. Attach the evidence: a number, a quote from a named participant (`P2`), a screenshot of the original. A problem without evidence is a claim.

### 4. What you found

Not the full research report. The finding that made you stop and change course. If no finding ever changed your direction, either you did not do research or you did not listen to it.

### 5. The design

Show decisions, not a tour of screens. For each: what you saw, what you chose, and what you rejected. Caption every image with what to notice. An uncaptioned screenshot is decoration.

### 6. A specific tradeoff

> "I chose a longer confirmation dialog that names exactly what will be lost, because the cost of an accidental cancellation was much higher than three extra seconds. It slows down users who know what they are doing, and I accepted that."

### 7. What testing changed

One before-and-after with a number or an observed behaviour. "In round one, 3 of 5 missed the pause option. I moved it from the footer to the plan card; in round two, 5 of 5 found it."

### 8. The outcome

If you shipped and measured, say what moved and whether it is distinguishable from noise. If you did not, say what you measured in testing and label it as such. Never claim a business result you did not measure.

### 9. What you got wrong

A real answer. A decision you would reverse, a test you ran badly, an assumption you carried too long. Without it the reader assumes you did not look.

## Three case studies, three different skills

Your portfolio shows three, not five. They must show range. Choose the ones with the strongest evidence, not the prettiest.

| Case study | Usually shows |
|---|---|
| The consumer showpiece (Sprint 3) | Visual craft, interaction, motion, and judgement under pressure |
| A research-led flow (Sprint 1 or 2) | Evidence, accessibility, and states |
| The capstone (Sprint 5) | Scoping, handling a failure, and a complete result |

The design system (Sprint 4) is a case study in its own right; use it instead of one of the above if the role is system-oriented.

---

## What not to include

- A persona you did not use
- A process diagram, unless the process was the innovation
- Screens you cannot defend
- Claims about results you did not measure
- AI-generated imagery presented as research

AI-assisted work carries the disclosure footer from `../03-Books-And-Resources.md`, section 8.

---

## The ninety-second test

Give the first screen of your portfolio to someone for ninety seconds, then ask three questions: What does this person do? What did they do on the best project? Would you keep reading? If they cannot answer the first two, the problem is the hook, not the work.
