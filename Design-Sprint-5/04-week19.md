# the start of the week

## LEARN — 60 minutes, self-directed
The right testing method for what you built. A moderated task-based test is the default, but if your key risk is comprehension it might be a comprehension test, and if it is findability it might be a first-click test.

→ **Commit** `students/UX{n}/week19/session1-learning.md`

## DO — 3 hours

### K3.1 — Test plan

**Method**
1. What you are testing, and what you are not.
2. Your tasks, written as realistic scenarios with no instructions embedded in them.
3. **What would falsify your design.** Write the specific observable behaviour that would tell you the design is wrong. Before you test.
4. Who, how many, and how recruited.
5. What you will do with each possible outcome.

Point 3 is the one that makes the test honest. If you cannot say in advance what failure looks like, you will interpret whatever happens as success.

**Worked example** — point 3:

> **My design assumes** that a user will understand "Reschedule" as the way to move an appointment rather than cancelling and re-booking.
> **What would falsify it:** two or more of five participants, when asked to move their appointment to a different day, tap "Cancel" first.
> **What I do if that happens:** the label is wrong, not the flow. I test "Change date" as an alternative, and if the concept itself is not landing I surface both actions on the appointment card with the consequences stated.

**Done when**
- [ ] Scope of the test stated
- [ ] Tasks written as scenarios with no embedded instructions
- [ ] A specific falsification condition per key assumption, written before testing
- [ ] Participants, count, and recruitment stated
- [ ] A planned response for each outcome

→ `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-1-test-plan.md`

# early in the week

## DO — 5 hours

### K3.2 — Run the sessions

**Method**
Five participants minimum. Real users from your target group, not peers.

The rules, which are the same rules and are harder now because it is your work: no leading, no explaining, no rescuing. When they get stuck, count to five before you intervene. When they say "I probably just missed it", write down where it was.

**Done when**
- [ ] Five or more sessions, consented and anonymised
- [ ] Notes straight away
- [ ] Every task's completion recorded, with the time and the errors
- [ ] Direct quotes captured verbatim
- [ ] Zero instances of you explaining the design mid-task

→ `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-2-sessions.md`

# mid week

## DO — 4 hours

### K3.3 — Findings
With denominators and severity. Every finding tagged as a comprehension problem, a discoverability problem, an interaction problem, or a content problem — because the fix differs by type.
→ `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-3-findings.md`

### K3.4 — Iteration plan

**Method**
1. Every finding gets a decision: fix now, fix later, or accept.
2. For "fix now": what specifically changes.
3. For "fix later": where it is recorded so it is not lost.
4. For "accept": why, and what evidence would change your mind.
5. **State which finding you least want to be true.** That is usually the important one, and naming it is the only reliable defence against quietly deprioritising it.

**Done when**
- [ ] Every finding has a decision
- [ ] Fixes are specific changes, not intentions
- [ ] Accepted findings have a reason and a reversal condition
- [ ] The finding you least want to be true is named

→ `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-4-iteration-plan.md`

# late in the week

## The failure injection lands

Sprint 5's injection is severe and it lands with one week left. It may invalidate a finding, remove a user group, impose a technical constraint that breaks your core interaction, or change a fixed constraint you built around.

## LEARN — 45 minutes, self-directed
Whatever the injection just made necessary.

→ **Commit** `session4-learning.md`

## DO — 4 hours (plus critique and whiteboard why)

### K3.5 — Iterate
Execute the fix-now list. Every change gets a decision record with the finding ID that caused it. **The trace from evidence to change is the deliverable**, not the change itself.
→ `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-5-iteration.md`

### X5 — Absorb the injection

**Method**
1. What changed.
2. What it invalidates in your work, specifically and honestly.
3. What you do about it in the remaining time.
4. What you cut to make room. Something has to go.
5. What you would have done differently in week 17 to be more resilient to this.

Point 5 is usually the same answer: I would have identified that constraint as assumed rather than fixed, and I would have designed the interaction so it did not depend on it.

**Done when**
- [ ] The change stated factually
- [ ] What it invalidates, without minimising
- [ ] A plan for the remaining time
- [ ] What you cut, named
- [ ] A specific week 17 action that would have helped

→ `students/UX{n}/week19/X5-injection.md`

### the week's critique — 90 minutes
Present the findings and the changes. The critique focus: **show the trace from a finding to a change.** A design that did not change after testing means either the testing was bad or you ignored it.

### Whiteboard why — 15 minutes
Anything from weeks 1 to 19. The whole program is in scope now.

# the end of the week

## DO — 3 hours

### K3.6 — Ethics review

**Method**
Review your own design with your own Sprint 3 ethics position in hand.
1. Where does your design use persuasion? Apply your own test.
2. Who could your design harm, and how? Be specific about which users.
3. What data does it collect, and does it need all of it?
4. Where does the user have less power than the operator, and what did you do about that?
5. **What in your own design would you refuse to ship if someone else had asked for it?** There is usually something, and finding it is the exercise.

**Done when**
- [ ] Your own E1.2 test applied to your own screens
- [ ] Harm assessed for specific user types
- [ ] Data collection justified item by item
- [ ] Power asymmetry named and addressed
- [ ] At least one thing in your own design identified as a problem you created

→ `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-6-ethics-review.md`

## Evidence gate — the end of the week build time

**What is checked:**
- Five or more real participants
- Findings with denominators and severity
- **Traceable changes** — a specific design change linked to a specific finding
- Accepted findings justified with reversal conditions
- The injection absorbed with a written plan
- The ethics review identifies a real problem in your own work

**The gate fails if nothing changed.** A design that survived testing untouched has almost always not been tested properly.
