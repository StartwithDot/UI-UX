**What to notice about GOV.UK:** a component is not accepted until it has been used in at least two services and has research evidence behind it. That single rule prevents most of the bloat that kills design systems, and it is why their component list is short and their components are all load-bearing.

→ **Commit** `students/UX{n}/week15/session1-learning.md`

## DO — 2.5 hours

### G1.1 — Governance model

**Method**
Write the model. Six sections.

1. **Who owns the system.** A named role, not a committee. Committees do not make decisions.
2. **Who can change what.** A table: tokens · core components · patterns · documentation · product-specific extensions. For each: who can propose, who reviews, who decides.
3. **What requires approval and what does not.** Be generous here. A system where everything needs approval gets bypassed, and a bypassed system is worse than no system.
4. **How disagreements resolve.** With an actual tiebreaker. "We discuss until we agree" is not a resolution mechanism.
5. **The escalation path**, with a time limit at each stage. A proposal that can sit unanswered forever is a proposal that gets forked instead.
6. **What happens when someone bypasses the process.** Because they will, and usually for a legitimate reason — a deadline. Your answer should not be punishment; it should be a path back in.

**Worked example** — section 6:

> **When someone bypasses the process.** The ops team shipped a custom date-range picker two days before a release rather than waiting for the system component. That is a reasonable decision under a deadline and I am not going to treat it as a violation.
>
> What happens: it goes on the **divergence register** within a week, with the reason, the owner, and a review date one month out. At the review, one of three things happens — it is absorbed into the system, it is accepted as a permanent product-specific exception with its own documentation, or it is migrated to the system component. The default if nobody attends the review is that it becomes a documented exception, because an undocumented fork is the only outcome I actually need to prevent.

Note what that does: it accepts reality, it creates a record, and its default outcome is still better than silence.

**Done when**
- [ ] All six sections
- [ ] The owner is a named role
- [ ] The change-rights table covers all five categories with all three columns
- [ ] There is a real tiebreaker
- [ ] Time limits at every escalation stage
- [ ] Section 6 provides a path back in rather than a punishment

→ `students/UX{n}/week15/G1-1-governance.md`

# Day 2

## LEARN — 75 minutes

| Source | What exactly | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **Any public RFC process** | Rust's or React's RFC repository. Read one accepted and one rejected RFC. | 40 min |
| **Polaris** | Their contribution documentation, specifically what they ask for before they will look at something | 20 min |

**Read a rejected RFC carefully.** The rejection comment is the artefact worth studying. A good rejection explains the reasoning, thanks the contributor, and leaves the door open. A bad one just says no, and it costs you every future contribution from that person.

→ **Commit** `session2-learning.md`

## DO — 2.5 hours

### G1.2 — Contribution process

**Method**
1. Design the path from "I need something" to "it is in the system".
2. Write the proposal template. It must ask for: the problem, the evidence that it is a real problem, what exists already and why it is insufficient, the proposed API, and the accessibility considerations.
3. Define the review stages and who is at each.
4. **State the time commitment at each stage**, both for the contributor and the reviewer. A process with unknown cost does not get used.
5. Design the fast path. Not everything needs the full process — a documentation fix or a new icon should take minutes. If your only path is the heavy one, people will route around it.

**Done when**
- [ ] The full path documented end to end
- [ ] A proposal template that asks for evidence, not just an idea
- [ ] Review stages with named participants
- [ ] Time cost stated for every stage
- [ ] A fast path exists, with clear criteria for what qualifies

→ `students/UX{n}/week15/G1-2-contribution.md`

### G1.3 — Rejection criteria

**Method**
1. Write the criteria for rejecting a contribution. Being able to reject is what makes a system a system.
2. Cover at least: it duplicates something that exists · it is too specific to one product · it has no evidence of need · it cannot be made accessible · it increases the API surface more than it reduces work · the maintenance cost exceeds the benefit.
3. For each, write the actual rejection message. Tone matters as much as the criterion, because a person you reject badly forks instead of contributing next time.
4. Every rejection must offer an alternative.

**Worked example**

> **Criterion: too product-specific.**
>
> **The message:** "Thanks for this — the problem is real and I can see it in your screenshots. I do not think it belongs in the core system, though: the vertical stepper only makes sense for the admin portal's multi-stage approval flow, and putting it in core means every other product carries the maintenance cost of something they will never use.
>
> Two options. One: build it in the doctor console's own component layer, using system tokens, and I will add it to the divergence register so it is documented and reviewable rather than invisible. Two: if the patient app or the admin portal finds a use for it in the next quarter, that is two products and it becomes a core candidate — bring it back and I will fast-track it.
>
> Either way, I would like to take the *state logic* you worked out into the core Stepper's documentation, because that part is general and it is better than what we have. Credit to you in the changelog."

Note: it accepts the problem as real, it gives the reason in terms of cost to others, it offers two concrete paths, it names the condition under which the answer changes, and it takes the part that was genuinely reusable.

**Done when**
- [ ] Six or more criteria
- [ ] A written rejection message for each
- [ ] Every message offers an alternative
- [ ] Every message names what would change the answer
- [ ] None of them reads as dismissive

→ `students/UX{n}/week15/G1-3-rejection.md`

## DO — 2.5 hours

### G1.4 — Deprecate something real

**Method**
1. Pick something in the cohort's system that should not exist. There will be candidates: a duplicate, an over-specific component, a badly named token, something from Sprint 1 that everyone has worked around since.
2. Write the deprecation:
   - **What is being removed and why**, in the first two sentences
   - **What replaces it**, with a side-by-side comparison
   - **The migration path**, step by step, with an honest effort estimate
   - **The timeline**: when it is marked deprecated, when it stops receiving fixes, when it is removed
   - **What breaks if someone ignores this**
   - **Who to talk to** if the migration does not work for them
3. Actually execute it. Mark it in the system, write the changelog entry, and post the communication.
4. **Get one peer to migrate their usage** and log what went wrong in their migration. Something will.

**Worked example** — the timeline section:

> | Date | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | 14 March | Marked deprecated in the library and the docs. Still works, still supported. A console warning is added. |
> | 14 April | No further bug fixes. Existing usage keeps working. |
> | 14 May | Removed from the library. Anything still using it breaks. |
>
> Two months from announcement to removal, with a month of warning before support stops. Shorter than that and teams with a release freeze cannot comply. Longer and everyone forgets and it becomes permanent.

**Done when**
- [ ] All six sections
- [ ] The migration path is step by step, with an effort estimate
- [ ] The timeline has three dated stages with reasoning
- [ ] Executed in the actual system, with a changelog entry
- [ ] One peer has migrated, and their problems are logged
- [ ] The communication is written and posted

→ `students/UX{n}/week15/G1-4-deprecation.md`

# Day 4

## LEARN — 45 minutes

Read the changelog of a real design system for the last six months. Note how they communicate breaking changes, and how much notice they give.

→ **Commit** `session4-learning.md`

## DO — 2 hours (plus critique and whiteboard why)

### G1.5 — Versioning policy

**Method**
1. Define what counts as major, minor, and patch **for a design system**, which is harder than for a library because visual change is not caught by a type checker.
2. The hard question: is a visual change breaking? A component that is 4px taller can break a layout. Decide, and state your rule.
3. State how you communicate each type, and where.
4. State your support window for the previous major version.

**Worked example** — the visual change rule:

> **My rule:** a visual change is breaking if it changes the component's outer dimensions, its baseline position, or its interactive target area. It is not breaking if it changes colour, weight, radius, or internal spacing that does not affect the outer box.
>
> **Why that line:** dimensional changes propagate into layouts that other people own. Colour changes do not. This means a colour correction ships as a patch and a padding increase ships as a major, which feels wrong until the first time someone's table row height changes overnight in production.

**Done when**
- [ ] Major, minor, and patch defined for design system changes specifically
- [ ] A stated rule for whether visual changes are breaking, with reasoning
- [ ] Communication channel and format stated per type
- [ ] A support window for the previous major

→ `students/UX{n}/week15/G1-5-versioning.md`

### G1.6 — Breaking change process

The full process for shipping a breaking change: the notice period, the migration guide requirement, whether a codemod or an automated migration is expected, and what happens to teams who cannot migrate in the window.

That last part matters. A team in a release freeze cannot migrate. Your process needs an answer other than "too bad".

→ `students/UX{n}/week15/G1-6-breaking-changes.md`

### Critique — 90 minutes
Present your rejection criteria. A peer brings a contribution and you reject it live, using your own messages. The critique focus: **would they contribute again after that rejection?** Ask them.

### Whiteboard why — 15 minutes
Draw your token architecture from memory, all three layers with real examples. Then name your rejection criteria without looking.

# Day 5

## The failure injection lands

In this sprint the injection is a governance failure. Something bypassed your process, or your process blocked something legitimate, or a team has quietly forked half your components.

## DO — 2 hours

### X4 — Post mortem

**Method**
1. What happened, factually.
2. **Which of your governance rules caused it.** Not "the team did not follow the process" — that is a symptom. Which rule made not following the process the rational choice for them?
3. What it cost.
4. What you change in the governance model, specifically.
5. What you deliberately do not change, and why. Not every failure means the rule was wrong.

Point 5 is important. Reacting to every incident by adding a rule is how governance models become the thing everyone bypasses.

**Done when**
- [ ] The facts, without blame
- [ ] A named rule of yours identified as the cause
- [ ] The cost stated
- [ ] A specific change to the model
- [ ] At least one thing you are deliberately not changing, with the reason

→ `students/UX{n}/week15/X4-post-mortem.md`
