# Day 1

## LEARN — 75 minutes

| Source | What exactly | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **Design Systems**, Kholmatova | Ch. 8 and the closing chapters, on systems in practice | 30 min |
| **Sparkbox** or any published design system survey | The sections on adoption measurement and on why systems fail | 25 min |
| **Figma / Zeroheight** | Any published article on measuring design system adoption | 20 min |

**What to take:** the most common cause of design system failure is not bad components. It is that using the system is slower than not using it for the person who has a deadline. Every adoption problem should be diagnosed against that sentence first.

→ **Commit** `students/UX{n}/week16/session1-learning.md`

## DO — 2 hours

### A1.1 — Adoption measurement plan

**Method**
1. Define what adoption means for your system. Be specific — "the team likes it" is not a measure.
2. Pick metrics you could actually collect: percentage of screens using system components, count of detached instances, count of components in a product's local library that duplicate a system component, time from proposal to merge, number of open divergences on the register.
3. For each: how you collect it, how often, and what number would trigger action.
4. **Add a guardrail.** Adoption can be gamed — a team can hit 100% component usage by wrapping your components in their own, or by using the wrong component because it is the only one available. Name what would show that.
5. State what you cannot measure, and what proxy you would use instead.

**Worked example**

| Metric | How collected | Frequency | Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| % of frames using library components | Figma analytics or a plugin audit | Monthly | Below 70% for a product → adoption interview with that team |
| Detached instances | Plugin audit | Monthly | Rising two months in a row → the component is missing something, investigate which |
| Proposal-to-merge time | The contribution log | Per proposal | Median above 3 weeks → the process is the bottleneck, not the contributors |

**Guardrail:** count of local components that wrap a system component. High component usage with high wrapping means teams are using the system as a base and fighting it, which reads as adoption in the numbers and is not adoption.

**Cannot measure:** whether the component was the *right* choice for the job. Proxy: the divergence register's growth rate, and the questions asked in the support channel.

**Done when**
- [ ] Adoption defined specifically
- [ ] Four or more collectable metrics with collection methods
- [ ] A trigger threshold for each
- [ ] A guardrail metric that would catch gaming
- [ ] What you cannot measure, with a stated proxy

→ `students/UX{n}/week16/A1-1-adoption-metrics.md`

# Day 2

## LEARN — 60 minutes

Read two published accounts of a design system migration — a company engineering blog is the usual source. Note what they underestimated. It is almost always the long tail of one-off screens.

→ **Commit** `session2-learning.md`

## DO — 2.5 hours

### A1.2 — Migration guide

**Method**
1. Pick one of the three products. Write the guide for moving it onto your system.
2. Sections: what changes · what breaks · the order to do it in · the effort estimate per stage · how to verify each stage · what to do when something has no equivalent.
3. **Order matters.** Tokens before components, because components consume tokens. Low-risk screens before high-traffic ones, so the team builds confidence before it faces the scary migration.
4. Give an honest effort estimate. An underestimated migration is abandoned halfway through, which leaves the product in a worse state than not starting.
5. The last section is the one that decides whether the guide gets used: **what to do when there is no system equivalent.** The answer must not be "wait for us".

**Worked example** — the ordering, with reasoning:

> **Stage 1 — tokens only, no component changes.** Swap hard-coded values for token references. Nothing should look different. This is the safest possible change and it makes every later stage cheaper. Estimate: 2 days. Verify: a visual diff should show zero changes.
>
> **Stage 2 — leaf components on low-traffic screens.** Buttons, inputs, and badges on the settings pages. Small blast radius, and it surfaces the API mismatches early where they cost nothing. Estimate: 3 days.
>
> **Stage 3 — layout and container components.** Cards, modals, and page shells. These change dimensions, so expect visual regressions. Estimate: 5 days, plus a review.
>
> **Stage 4 — the data table.** Last, deliberately. It is the highest-traffic component, it has the most product-specific behaviour, and by now the team knows the system well enough to argue with me about it properly. Estimate: 5 days, and I expect two API change requests out of this stage.

**Done when**
- [ ] All six sections
- [ ] Stages ordered with a stated reason for the order
- [ ] Effort estimated per stage, honestly
- [ ] Verification method per stage
- [ ] The no-equivalent section gives the team a path that does not involve waiting

→ `students/UX{n}/week16/A1-2-migration-guide.md`

# Day 3

## LEARN — 45 minutes

Re-read your own Sprint 2 interviewing notes and your R4.2 self-critique. You are about to interview people about your own work, which is the hardest kind of interview to run without leading.

→ **Commit** `session3-learning.md`

## DO — 2.5 hours

### A1.3 — Adoption interviews

**Method**
1. Interview three peers who have used your components. Real interviews, Sprint 2 protocol.
2. **You are interviewing about your own work, which means your bias is at its maximum.** Do not defend. Do not explain what you intended. When they describe something as confusing, your only job is to find out what they expected instead.
3. Ask what they did when the system did not have what they needed. That answer is the whole finding.
4. Ask what they would fork if they had a deadline tomorrow.

**Done when**
- [ ] Three interviews, notes same day
- [ ] Zero instances of you defending a decision — check the transcript
- [ ] The what-did-you-do-instead question asked of all three
- [ ] The deadline question asked of all three

→ `students/UX{n}/week16/A1-3-adoption-interviews.md`

### A1.4 — Diagnosis

**Method**
1. For every case where someone did not use the system, find the actual cause. The honest categories: they did not know it existed · they could not find it · the documentation did not answer their question · it did not do what they needed · it was faster to build their own · they disagreed with the design.
2. **Each of those has a different fix, and only the last one is about design.** The first three are documentation and discovery problems, the fourth is a gap, the fifth is a process problem.
3. For each cause, name the fix and who owns it.
4. Rank by how many people it affects.

**Done when**
- [ ] Every non-adoption case categorised
- [ ] A fix named per cause, with an owner
- [ ] Ranked by reach
- [ ] At least one case where the cause is something you did, stated plainly

→ `students/UX{n}/week16/A1-4-diagnosis.md`

# Day 4

## LEARN — 45 minutes

Read how any real system reports its own health publicly — a status page, a public roadmap, a quarterly report. Note what they choose to show and what they leave out.

→ **Commit** `session4-learning.md`

## DO — 2 hours (plus critique)

### A1.5 — System health dashboard

**Method**
1. One page. Everything about the system's health, readable in two minutes.
2. Include: component count and how many are documented to standard · adoption per product · open divergences · deprecations in flight · open proposals with their age · accessibility conformance per component · debt items with their age.
3. **Show the bad numbers.** A dashboard that only shows what is going well is marketing. The debt column with a four-sprint-old item on it is the most useful cell on the page.

**Done when**
- [ ] All seven categories present
- [ ] Readable in two minutes
- [ ] The bad numbers are visible, not buried
- [ ] Committed as Markdown, so it can be diffed over time

→ `students/UX{n}/week16/A1-5-health.md`

# Day 5

## Defense — 60 minutes

| Part | Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Token architecture | 10 min | Three layers from memory, with real examples |
| Component API | 10 min | One component's API, every property justified |
| Governance | 10 min | Who decides what, and how something gets rejected |
| Deprecation | 10 min | What you removed, how, and who it inconvenienced |
| Adoption | 10 min | Your real numbers, including the bad ones, and your diagnosis |
| Alternative | 10 min | A constraint card is drawn |

**The adoption section is where most people lose marks.** The failure mode is defending the system instead of diagnosing it. If two teams forked your components, the answer is not "they should have asked" — it is "here is what made forking the rational choice, and here is what I changed".

**Sprint 4 constraint cards:** the system must support a fourth product with a completely different brand · one team refuses to adopt and has executive backing · you must cut the component count in half · the engineering team will only consume tokens, not components · the system must ship to an external partner with no support channel.

## P16.1 — Reflection
- The API decision you got wrong, and how you know
- The governance rule that was bypassed, and why bypassing it was rational
- What you learned from the deprecation that you could not have learned from building
- The defense question you answered worst
- One behaviour you will change in Sprint 5

→ `students/UX{n}/week16/P16-1-reflection.md`
