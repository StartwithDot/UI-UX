# The Ethics Position

The framework for the week 12 refusal task, and what a defensible line looks like. Read it before week 11; use it in week 12.

---

## What it is for

A design ethics position is a short, specific statement of where you will and will not go, written before the pressure arrives. Its job is to be usable in a meeting with a manager who wants the number to go up. A position that cannot be tested in that meeting is a slogan.

---

## The five sections

Your position has exactly the five sections task E2.1 asks for, one paragraph or list each.

### 1. What I will not do
Specific practices, named, each one pointable-at on a screen. Keep the list short, at most five, so each item is believable. "Deceive users" is too vague. "Design a cancellation path with more required steps than the sign-up path" is a line you can be held to.

### 2. What I will do under protest
The grey area. "Under protest" must be a concrete action, usually: I build it, and I put my objection in the decision log with my name on it and ask for the risk owner to be named. State which kinds of request you would handle this way and which conditions (a written decision, a legal review, a time limit, a way to measure harm) you would need first.

### 3. What I consider acceptable persuasion
Your test from E1.2, applied. Persuasion survives being explained to the user; manipulation does not. Give two examples that pass your test and two that fail, from real products.

### 4. How I would raise a concern
The actual path: who you tell first, in what order, in writing or in person, what you say, what you offer instead, and what you do if nothing changes. A refusal that offers no alternative and names no business cost is a protest, not a negotiation.

### 5. What I would need in order to refuse
Honest numbers. A difficult conversation, a bad review, a lost promotion, a lost job. And what you would need (savings, seniority, a second offer, a union, a written policy) for the line to hold under that cost. This section is graded for honesty, not for courage.

---

## A defensible line has four properties

| Property | Test |
|---|---|
| **Specific** | Could you point at a design and say "that crosses it"? |
| **Testable** | Could a colleague apply it to a case you have not thought of and reach the same answer? |
| **Costed** | Does it say what holding it costs the business, and you? |
| **Honest about exceptions** | Does it say when it bends? A position with no stated exceptions is one you will break without noticing. |

---

## Three ways it goes wrong

**Grandstanding.** "I would resign before shipping a dark pattern." Believable from nobody who has not yet been in the room. The examiner will say so.

**Collapse.** "I'd do what the business asked." Accepted as a description of a fact, not as a position.

**Vagueness.** "I'd always put the user first." Cannot be violated, so it cannot be held.

---

## The test case you are given

In week 11 you build a manipulative cancellation flow (E1.3) and a business demand arrives from the client. In week 12 you write the refusal (E2.2), under 300 words, as if your manager had asked you to ship the confirmshaming and the fake timer. It must contain: what you will not do, the reason in terms the manager cares about, the alternative you will build with an honest cost, what you will do instead of nothing, and what happens if the decision goes against you.
