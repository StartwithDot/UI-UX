# Day 1

## LEARN — 90 minutes

| Source | What exactly | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **Designing Interface Animation**, Val Head | Ch. 1–3: what animation is for, the five roles of animation in UI | 50 min |
| **Material 3** | The motion overview and the "transition patterns" pages | 25 min |
| **Apple HIG** | The Motion section | 15 min |

**What to take:** animation has a small number of legitimate jobs — showing where something came from, showing what changed, keeping a user oriented during a layout change, indicating progress, and directing attention to something that just appeared. Anything that does not do one of those five is decoration, and decoration on a billing screen has a cost with no benefit.

→ **Commit** `students/UX{n}/week10/session1-learning.md`

## DO — 2 hours

### I2.1 — Motion audit

**Method**
1. Pick three real products you use. One should be a payment or banking app, where motion is usually conservative for good reasons.
2. Screen-record five transitions in each.
3. For each, identify: what it communicates · roughly how long it takes · whether it helps or delays · whether you would keep it.
4. Find one animation in each product that exists purely as decoration, and say what removing it would cost.
5. Find one that genuinely helps, and say what removing it would cost.

**Worked example**

> **App:** A payments app, the "send money" confirmation.
> **What it communicates:** the amount card lifts and moves toward the recipient's avatar, which shows the direction of the transfer and which account it went to. About 400ms.
> **Helps or delays:** helps. It answers "did it go to the right person" without the user reading anything.
> **Would I keep it:** yes, but I would shorten it to 300ms. At 400ms it is noticeable on repeat use, and this is a repeated action.

**Done when**
- [ ] Three products, five transitions each
- [ ] Every one has a stated communicated meaning, or is marked as decoration
- [ ] Rough durations noted
- [ ] One decorative and one useful example named per product, with the cost of removal
- [ ] Recordings or frame sequences committed

→ `students/UX{n}/week10/I2-1-motion-audit.md`

# Day 2

## LEARN — 75 minutes

| Source | What exactly | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **Designing Interface Animation** | The chapters on timing and easing | 35 min |
| **easings.net** | Look at each curve and its shape. Understand ease-out versus ease-in-out. | 20 min |
| **Material 3** | The "Easing and duration" specification page | 20 min |

**The rule you need:** things entering the screen use ease-out, because they should arrive quickly and settle. Things leaving use ease-in, because they should start slowly and accelerate away. Things moving within the screen use ease-in-out. Linear is for things that genuinely have constant speed, like a progress bar or a spinner, and for nothing else.

→ **Commit** `session2-learning.md`

## DO — 2 hours

### I2.2 — Duration and easing decisions

**Method**
1. List every animation you intend to have in your flow.
2. For each, assign a duration. Use the bands: 100–150ms for small state changes on a control · 200–300ms for entering and exiting elements · 300–400ms for a full-screen or layout transition · above 400ms only with a written justification.
3. For each, assign an easing curve and state why that curve for that motion.
4. Anything above 400ms needs a specific reason. "It looks nicer" is not one.

**Worked example**

| Animation | Duration | Easing | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Button pressed state | 100ms | ease-out | Must feel immediate. Any longer and the button feels unresponsive. |
| Cancellation confirmation dialog entering | 250ms | ease-out | Enters quickly and settles. Long enough to see where it came from, short enough not to delay a user who has already decided. |
| Plan comparison expanding | 300ms | ease-in-out | The layout is changing height, and the user needs to track what moved. Ease-in-out reads as a single continuous motion. |
| Progress bar during payment | continuous | linear | It represents actual elapsed progress. Easing would misrepresent the rate. |

**Done when**
- [ ] Every animation listed
- [ ] Duration assigned within the bands, or justified above them
- [ ] Easing assigned with a reason per animation
- [ ] Linear used only where the motion genuinely has constant rate

→ `students/UX{n}/week10/I2-2-timing.md`

### I2.3 — Purpose test

**Method**
1. Go back through your list and for each animation answer: which of the five legitimate roles does it perform?
2. If the answer is "none", delete it.
3. **Delete at least two.** If you cannot find two to delete, you have not been honest, because everyone over-animates on their first motion spec.
4. For each deletion, say what you thought it was doing and why you were wrong.

**Done when**
- [ ] Every remaining animation mapped to one of the five roles
- [ ] At least two deleted
- [ ] Each deletion explained
- [ ] Nothing survives on the grounds that it looks good

→ `students/UX{n}/week10/I2-3-purpose-test.md`

## DO — 2.5 hours

### I2.4 — Build the transitions

**Method**
1. Build your surviving animations in Figma using Smart Animate, with the exact durations and curves from I2.2.
2. Record each one as a video or GIF and commit it. A written spec of motion is not reviewable; a recording is.
3. **Watch each one ten times in a row.** Everything looks fine once. Repetition is how you find the animation that is 100ms too slow, and repetition is also what your real users experience.
4. Adjust and note every change you made after watching, with the reason.

**Done when**
- [ ] Every surviving animation built and recorded
- [ ] Durations and curves match I2.2, or the deviation is logged
- [ ] Each watched at least ten times
- [ ] Every adjustment logged with the reason
- [ ] Figma prototype link plus committed recordings

# Day 4

## LEARN — 60 minutes

| Source | What exactly | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **WCAG 2.3.3** | Animation from Interactions, in full | 15 min |
| **WCAG 2.2.2** | Pause, Stop, Hide | 15 min |

**What to take:** for some people, large-area motion and parallax cause genuine nausea and dizziness. This is not a preference. `prefers-reduced-motion` exists because of it, and respecting it is a requirement rather than a courtesy.

→ **Commit** `session4-learning.md`

## DO — 2 hours (plus critique)

### I2.5 — Reduced motion

**Method**
1. For every animation, define the reduced-motion alternative.
2. **Reduced motion does not mean no motion.** It means no large-area movement, no parallax, no scaling of big elements, no spinning. A cross-fade is usually acceptable and preserves the sense of change.
3. For each, state whether reduced motion loses any information. If it does, say how you compensate — usually with text, or with a layout cue.
4. Anything that only communicates through motion is a design problem. Motion must be a reinforcement, never the only channel.

**Worked example**

| Animation | Full motion | Reduced motion | Information lost? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dialog entering | Scales from 95% and slides up 12px, 250ms ease-out | Opacity 0 to 1, 150ms | No. The dialog's presence is the information, and the fade still conveys arrival. |
| Plan card expanding | Height animates, contents stagger in, 300ms | Height changes instantly, contents fade in 100ms | Slight. The user loses the sense of what pushed down. Compensated by keeping the expanded card's border highlighted for 1s. |
| Success checkmark drawing | Path draws over 400ms | Checkmark appears at full opacity with the text | No. The text "Payment received" carries the meaning; the drawing was reinforcement. |

**Done when**
- [ ] Every animation has a reduced-motion alternative
- [ ] No alternative is simply "removed" unless the animation was decoration
- [ ] Information loss assessed per animation, with compensation where needed
- [ ] Nothing in the flow communicates solely through motion

→ `students/UX{n}/week10/I2-5-reduced-motion.md`

### C11.1 — Motion spec

**Method**
Write the spec an engineer would implement from. Per animation: the property that changes, the from and to values, the duration, the easing curve as a cubic-bezier or a named token, any delay, the trigger, and the reduced-motion variant.

Group repeated values into motion tokens — `duration-fast`, `duration-medium`, `easing-standard` — and use the tokens in the spec. If you have eleven different durations, you do not have a system.

**Done when**
- [ ] Every animation fully specified with all seven attributes
- [ ] Easing given as cubic-bezier values or named tokens, not as adjectives
- [ ] Motion tokens defined and used
- [ ] Reduced-motion variant included in the same spec, not in a separate document

→ `students/UX{n}/week10/C11-1-motion-spec.md`

### Critique — 90 minutes
Present three animations and their reduced-motion versions. The critique focus: **for each animation, what does the user not know if you delete it?** If the answer is "nothing", it should already have been deleted.

## DO — 2 hours

### I2.6 — Orientation and continuity

**Method**
1. Walk your whole flow as a sequence and ask at each step: does the user know where they came from and where they are?
2. Find every place where something appears with no spatial origin, or where the layout jumps.
3. Fix them using continuity: shared element transitions, consistent entry directions, a stable header.
4. State your directional rule and hold it. If forward is right-to-left, backward is left-to-right, everywhere, with no exceptions. Inconsistent direction is worse than no motion.

**Done when**
- [ ] Full flow walked as a sequence
- [ ] Every unexplained appearance or layout jump identified
- [ ] A stated directional rule, applied consistently
- [ ] No place where a user cannot tell what just happened

→ `students/UX{n}/week10/I2-6-continuity.md`
