# Week 11 — Dark Patterns

**Sprint 3 · Week 11 of 12 · Theme: build the manipulative version, then measure what it costs**

---

## This week is uncomfortable on purpose

You are going to design something that harms users, competently, and then document the harm. The reason is simple: you cannot reliably recognise manipulation in your own work if the only examples you have ever studied were somebody else's.

Everything you build this week stays in the repository, clearly labelled. Nothing goes on your portfolio without its documentation attached.

---

## By the end of this week you can

- Name the mechanism behind a dark pattern, not just point at it
- Tell the difference between persuasion and manipulation with a stated test
- Build a manipulative flow and account for exactly what it takes from the user
- Absorb a business demand that conflicts with your own principles

## DO — 2 hours

### E1.1 — Dark pattern audit

**Method**
1. Take three real products. One must be a subscription you have actually cancelled — you have the screenshots.
2. Find every dark pattern in the sign-up, the billing, and the cancellation flow.
3. For each: name the pattern from the taxonomy · describe the exact mechanism · state what it takes from the user, quantified where possible · state what the business gains.
4. **The mechanism is the important column.** "It is manipulative" is a label. "It exploits the fact that a user scanning for the exit reads only the high-contrast elements, and the exit is the only low-contrast element on the screen" is a mechanism, and it tells you how to detect the same trick elsewhere.

**Worked example**

> **Product:** a streaming service's cancellation flow.
> **Pattern:** obstruction, specifically roach motel — sign-up is two clicks, cancellation is seven screens.
> **Mechanism:** each screen presents a single "are you sure" variant with a different framing — price, content, family members, a retention offer. None is individually outrageous. The cumulative effect exploits decision fatigue: each screen has a small probability of the user giving up, and seven small probabilities compound into a large retention number.
> **What it takes from the user:** roughly 4 minutes, and for some proportion of users, a subscription they intended to end. If 8% abandon the cancellation, 8% are paying for something they decided to stop paying for.
> **What the business gains:** measurable retention, which is why it survives review. Someone has an dashboard showing this flow "working".

**Done when**
- [ ] Three products, sign-up and billing and cancellation covered
- [ ] Every finding named from the taxonomy
- [ ] Every finding has a described mechanism, not just a label
- [ ] User cost quantified where numbers are possible
- [ ] Business gain stated for each, because that is why it exists
- [ ] Screenshots as evidence

→ `students/UX{n}/week11/E1-1-dark-pattern-audit.md`

## DO — 2 hours

### E1.2 — Persuasion versus manipulation

**Method**
1. Write your own test for distinguishing the two. It has to be usable on a specific screen, by you, in a meeting, in ten seconds.
2. Apply it to ten real examples: five you consider acceptable persuasion, five you consider manipulation.
3. Find the two hardest cases, where your test gives an unclear answer. Say what makes them hard.
4. A test that classifies all ten cleanly is probably too crude. The boundary cases are where the thinking is.

**Worked example** — one possible test, and you should write your own:

> **My test, three questions:**
> 1. Would the user, if they understood exactly what this design is doing and why, still be fine with it?
> 2. Does it work *because* of a limitation in their attention, memory, or emotional state, rather than despite it?
> 3. Does it become less effective if I explain it to them?
>
> If question 3 is yes, it is manipulation. Persuasion survives being explained. "This plan is cheaper per month if you pay annually" works just as well when I tell you I am trying to get you onto annual billing. "Are you sure? Your kids will be disappointed" stops working the instant I say "we put that there because guilt reduces cancellations by 6%".
>
> **Hard case:** a genuine countdown on a genuinely limited offer. It exploits urgency, which is a limitation, but the urgency is real. My test gives an unclear answer. I resolve it by asking whether the limit is real and verifiable — if the same "offer ends in 3 hours" appears every day, the limit is fictional and it is manipulation.

**Done when**
- [ ] Your own test, usable in ten seconds
- [ ] Ten examples classified
- [ ] At least two hard cases where your test is unclear, with an explanation
- [ ] A stated way of resolving the hard cases, or an honest statement that you cannot

## DO — 2.5 hours

### E1.3 — Build the dark pattern

**Method**
Design a cancellation flow whose explicit goal is to maximise retention. Include all five:

1. **Confirmshaming** — an emotionally loaded rejection option
2. **Obstruction** — more steps than necessary, each individually justifiable
3. **Misdirection** — visual hierarchy that hides the exit in plain sight
4. **Fake urgency or scarcity** — a retention offer with a timer
5. **A pre-checked box** — something opted in by default that should not be

Build it properly. High fidelity. Using the design system. **Make it good**, because a badly built dark pattern teaches you nothing — the whole danger of these is that they look like competent, polished, professional design.

Label the file clearly at the top: `⚠️ DELIBERATE DARK PATTERN — see E1.4 for the cost accounting. Not for portfolio use without that document attached.`

**Done when**
- [ ] All five patterns present and identified in an annotation layer
- [ ] Built to full fidelity using the system
- [ ] It genuinely would work — a real user would plausibly be caught by it
- [ ] Labelled at the top of the file
- [ ] Figma link plus exports

## DO — 2 hours (plus critique and whiteboard why)

### E1.4 — Cost accounting

**Method**
For each of the five patterns you built, write:
1. **Who it harms.** Be specific about which users. Some of these harm the confused disproportionately; some harm the hurried; some harm people with cognitive disabilities much more than others.
2. **What it takes.** Quantified. Money, time, or data.
3. **The estimated effect.** Your honest guess at the retention lift, and what it is based on.
4. **The regulatory exposure.** Which of the CMA or FTC practice types it maps to.
5. **What you would say** if a manager asked you to ship it. The actual sentences, not a summary of your position.

**Worked example** — point 5 done properly:

> "I can build the timer, but I need to tell you what it exposes us to. The FTC's report names fictional urgency as a deceptive practice, and the CMA lists it under pressure-selling. If the offer is genuinely time-limited, I will build it and put the real expiry on it. If it resets every time the user reloads, that is a false claim in the interface and I am not comfortable putting my name on it. What I would build instead is the retention offer with no timer, presented once, with the real terms. My estimate is that it converts a little less, and it does not create a compliance question. If you still want the timer, I want the decision recorded in writing by whoever owns the risk, and I want the reset behaviour reviewed by legal."

Note what that does: it does not moralise, it names the specific exposure, it offers an alternative, it concedes the commercial cost, and it escalates the decision rather than absorbing it silently.

**Done when**
- [ ] All five patterns accounted for
- [ ] Harm attributed to specific user types, not "users"
- [ ] Costs quantified
- [ ] Each mapped to a named regulatory practice type
- [ ] Point 5 is written as actual sentences you would say

→ `students/UX{n}/week11/E1-4-cost-accounting.md`

### E1.5 — The honest version

**Method**
1. Redesign the same cancellation flow to be honest: fastest reasonable path out, clear consequences, one retention offer presented once, nothing pre-checked.
2. Estimate what it costs the business relative to your dark version.
3. State the argument for the honest version **in business terms**. Not ethical terms.
4. Point 3 is the real deliverable. "It is the right thing to do" loses the meeting. Reduced support contacts, reduced chargebacks, reduced regulatory exposure, reduced reputational risk, and higher reactivation rates from people who left on good terms all win it.

**Worked example** — the business argument:

> The honest flow costs roughly 5% of the retention the obstructive flow produces. Against that: users who cancel cleanly reactivate at a materially higher rate than users who feel tricked, because reactivation requires trust and the obstructive flow spends it. Support contacts about "I thought I cancelled" disappear entirely. Card-network chargebacks for unwanted renewals, which carry both a fee and a ratio penalty, drop. And the flow stops being the thing a journalist screenshots. The 5% is real and I am not pretending otherwise; I am saying it is the most expensive 5% we could book.

**Done when**
- [ ] Full honest redesign at the same fidelity as the dark version
- [ ] Business cost estimated honestly, not minimised
- [ ] The argument made in business terms with at least four distinct commercial reasons
- [ ] No appeal to ethics in point 3 — save that for the ethics position in week 12

→ `students/UX{n}/week11/E1-5-honest-version.md`

### the week's critique — 90 minutes
Present both versions side by side. The critique focus: **is your honest version actually honest, or is it the dark version with the timer removed?** Softened manipulation is still manipulation.

# the end of the week

## The failure injection lands

In this sprint the injection is a business demand. It will conflict with something in your P9.1 or P10.1 principles. That is the design of it.

## DO — 2 hours

### X3 — Post mortem

**Method**
1. What was demanded, factually.
2. Which of your principles it conflicts with, by number.
3. What you did. Complied, refused, negotiated, or found a third option.
4. What it cost either way.
5. What would have made this easier to handle. Usually: having stated the principle earlier, in writing, where someone else could see it.

**Done when**
- [ ] The demand stated factually, without editorialising
- [ ] The specific principle conflict named by number
- [ ] What you actually did, honestly, including if you complied
- [ ] The cost of your choice
- [ ] A named earlier action that would have made this easier

→ `students/UX{n}/week11/X3-post-mortem.md`

### P11.1 — Principle conflict

**Method**
1. Take the principle that was challenged.
2. Decide: does it hold, bend, or break?
3. If it bends, state the exact conditions under which it bends. A principle with unstated exceptions is not a principle.
4. If it broke, say so honestly. Then say what you would need — seniority, a written policy, another job offer — for it to have held.

Point 4 is not a failure. A junior designer who says "I complied and here is what I would have needed to refuse" is being more useful than one who claims they would have walked out.

**Done when**
- [ ] A clear decision: holds, bends, or breaks
- [ ] If it bends, the conditions are written explicitly
- [ ] If it broke, that is stated without self-justification
- [ ] What you would have needed, named concretely

→ `students/UX{n}/week11/P11-1-principle-conflict.md`
