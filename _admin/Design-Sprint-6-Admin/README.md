# Design Sprint 6, Admin

Private. General machinery is in `../Design-Sprint-1-Admin/`. This covers the portfolio, interview and market sprint, and the close of the program.

---

## What this sprint is testing

Whether the evidence of twenty weeks survives contact with strangers who have hiring authority, and whether the designer can talk about it honestly: strengths, gaps, and the parts that went wrong.

## Before week 21

- **Check every designer's JR drafts.** Anyone arriving without JR2 to JR5 is behind, and the sprint will be four weeks of catching up. Decide in week 20 whether they need a plan.
- **Book the external panel in week 20.** Three people if possible, two minimum, for the week 22 portfolio review, the week 24 mock loop and the defense. They must not have read the work and must not know the cohort. Brief them on one thing only: ask what you would actually ask, and do not be kind. See `guide/external-bench.md`.
- Build the list of **ten real job openings** the cohort could apply to, so nobody applies to nothing.
- Prepare the **placement-readiness rubric** (`rubrics/placement-readiness.md`) and make sure every external has read it.

## Weekly focus

**Week 21.** Read B1.1 first. The selection reasoning is the signal: whether they cut on evidence or on looks. The capstone is almost always one. Watch for three case studies that show the same skill three times. In the cut pass, the best rewrites cut the process theatre and keep the failure.

**Week 22.** The external review is the sprint's injection. A stranger will tell them the portfolio is not good enough. Watch how they respond in the first ten seconds. The ones who start explaining are defending; the ones who ask a question are learning. Check the first screen against the ninety-second test, and check the accessibility and performance numbers yourself rather than trusting the report.

**Week 23.** Read B3.1 for specifics: every project answer needs a number. The critique performance is graded on whether they are neither soft nor unpleasant. Listen to the recordings. The most common failure is a good answer delivered too fast and too long; the fix is to say it aloud and cut it in half.

**Week 24.** The three-hour mock loop is run by externals, not by you. Do not intervene. The ten applications are real: check they were sent, to the openings on the list, with a tailored first line. The 90-day plan is the last artefact; a plan with no review date in a real calendar is a wish.

## Grading honesty in the case studies

This is the part that requires discipline from the admin.

| Case study | Score |
|---|---|
| Measured result, sample stated, limits stated, a real mistake named | Strong |
| Unmeasured, labelled as an estimate, a real mistake named | Solid |
| Unmeasured, presented as a win | Weak. Say so plainly. |
| Sample too small to distinguish from noise, presented as a win | Weakest. This is the one to be hard on. |
| No mistake anywhere in the case study | Send back. It was written by someone who did not look. |

The last two rows are the important ones. A designer who presents an indistinguishable result as a success has learned to produce the appearance of rigour, which is worse than having no rigour, because it is harder to detect and it will follow them into their career.

## The final defense

90 minutes, run as an interview. Structure in `../../Design-Sprint-6/01-project-brief.md`. The external interviewer leads; you observe and do not intervene, even when a question is unfair. Unfair questions are the ones that happen in real rooms.

**The reflection round is the last thing in the program**, and it is the one to give its full five minutes. The question: "what are you still bad at." A designer who says "I have learned so much" has been present but not looking, and after 24 weeks that is worth saying to them directly, kindly, and once.

## The placement-readiness check

Run it at the end of the mock loop and the defense. Each external completes the rubric independently; you compare them afterwards and deliver the outcome to the designer, in person, with the written list of next steps. The three outcomes (Ready, Ready with a named gap, Not yet) are defined in `../../06-Job-Readiness-Track.md` section 5. "Not yet" is a result, not a failure. Do not soften it: the whole value of the check is that it is honest before the designer finds out from fifty rejections.

## The close

**Per designer, before the end of week 24:**
1. Final ledger entry, including the Sprint 6 trajectory call
2. The honest conversation: their strongest capability, their real gap, and what a hiring manager will find
3. What you would say in a reference, read to them, so they know
4. The 30, 90 and 180-day follow-up dates in the ledger (`guide/outcomes-tracking.md`)

**Cohort level:**
1. Design system handover written, so the next cohort inherits it with its governance intact
2. Program retro in `guide/decision-log.md`: which sprint produced the least learning per hour, which gate never worked, which injection did not land, which lab was not worth its hours, and what you fixed for them that you should not have
3. Review `../../09-Modern-Practice-Map.md` against the field: what changed in the last twelve months, what has died
4. The task cuts for the next cohort, decided while it is still fresh

## The failure specific to this sprint

The first is the **flinch**. A designer who did good work, got a hard external review or a "Not yet", and writes it up defensively, explaining it away rather than reading it. The whole program was pointed at that moment. If they flinch there, the honest ledger entry says so, because it is the thing that will most limit them in five years.

The second is the **gallery**: a beautiful portfolio with no argument in it. It looks good in week 22 and loses in the first screening.

The third is **applying to nothing**. Ten applications is the minimum, and the target list exists so nobody can say they did not know where. Check the sent dates.
