# Design Sprint 3, Admin

Private. General machinery is in `../Design-Sprint-1-Admin/`. This covers Sprint 3 only: Sutra, the subscription flow, interaction, motion, metrics and ethics.

---

## What this sprint is testing

Whether a designer can reason about money without abandoning the user, and whether they can hold a line under commercial pressure with an alternative rather than a refusal. It is also the program's visual showpiece, so look at craft with a hiring manager's eye.

## Before week 9

- Book the **external bench** for JR3 (live portfolio review and Mock interview 1) in week 12. See `guide/external-bench.md`.
- Seal the week 11 request. Choose which of the four requests in the brief (section 9) each designer receives. Giving everyone the same one lets them compare notes; give at least two different ones.
- Check that every designer has cancelled a real subscription and screenshotted it. Anyone who has not will have no material for week 9.

## Weekly focus

**Week 9.** Two things matter. First, the state machine: read the guards. "User can pause" is not a guard. Count the impossible combinations they found; fewer than three means they did not look. Second, **P9.2**: the brief's retention data says five lessons in week one lifts month-3 retention from 54% to 81%. A designer who defines activation as "signed up" or "started a trial" read the funnel and not the retention data. Watch for HEART tables with all five rows filled and three of them invented. An honest "we cannot measure happiness with what this company has" is stronger than a fabricated NPS row.

**Week 10.** The purpose test (I2.3) is where decoration dies. Anyone who deleted nothing did not apply it. Check the reduced-motion version: if it is "instant", they have not designed it. L-PR2 is the first time they hand a prototype to a stranger and stay silent; read the hesitation logs.

**Week 11.** The business demand arrives on Day 5 morning, alongside the post mortem. Do not preview which request it will be. What you are grading is not the answer, it is the structure of the reasoning: the number, the user cost, the regulatory position, the decision, the alternative. A refusal with no alternative scores the same as compliance with no argument, because both leave the client with the problem and neither is a design contribution.

**Week 12.** The experiment design in E1.5 and the instrumentation in the spec are where the sprint either landed or did not. An experiment with no minimum detectable effect means they do not understand what a result is. The brief gives about 3,400 cancellation attempts a month with 40% seeing an offer; a designer who proposes an effect too small to detect in that traffic, and does not notice, has not done the arithmetic. The showpiece pass (C12.3): look at the three screens for ten seconds each. Would you click through to the case study?

## Defense notes for this sprint

**Run the pressure round as the founder.** Sixteen months of runway, a board member who wants friction on the cancel button, and a design that wants six engineers for six weeks.

> "Convince me this is worth a third of my remaining engineering capacity. And before you answer, my last designer told me their work would improve retention and it did nothing."

**What to listen for:** whether they can talk about money without either flinching from it or abandoning the user for it. Both failures are common and they look different. The flincher retreats to "good experience". The abandoner agrees to everything and produces the dark pattern.

**The follow-up for anyone who refused cleanly:** "so you have told me no, my churn problem is unchanged, and I have sixteen months. What am I supposed to do." A designer with an alternative ready is doing the job. A designer with only a principle is not yet useful to a company that has to survive.

## The failure specific to this sprint

Designers who treat business constraints as an imposition rather than as part of the problem. It shows up as a cut list with nothing on it, or a business case that assumes the design will work.

The other failure, less common, is the designer who enjoys the commercial framing so much that the dark pattern audit comes back soft. Nobody's design has zero persuasive elements. An empty audit means they stopped looking at the point where looking became uncomfortable.

The third is cosmetic: a showpiece that fails contrast or ignores reduced motion. Send it back. The standard sits above the gate, not beside it.
